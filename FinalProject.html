Introduction

California’s history with earthquakes has been marked by devastating events that reshaped building practices and public safety. The 1906 San Francisco earthquake stands as the deadliest, killing around 3,000 people and destroying most of the city, while the 1994 Northridge earthquake caused enormous economic damage despite its smaller magnitude.

Today, much of California’s population lives close to active faults such as the San Andreas and Hayward, facing significant ongoing risk and billions in potential annual losses. Residents are encouraged to stay prepared through tools like early warning apps and earthquake drills, especially as scientists warn the current “seismic drought” could end suddenly.

This interactive map provides a focused look at California’s complex seismic landscape by centering on the state's coordinates with a broad, high-level zoom. Built using the Mapbox GL JS framework, the visualization features a custom geographic style that highlights the physical terrain. My visualization serves to create context explaining why California is prone to earthquakes. By presenting the state in its tectonic entirety, the map allows viewers to see the sheer density of geological features that define the region's active landscape.

The map specifically utilizes a vector tileset to render offshore fault lines, colored in a distinct green to separate them from the surrounding seafloor. Interspersed across the map are large, stylized arrows (blue pointing northwest and magenta pointing southeast) that represent the relative motion of the Pacific and North American plates. Showing fault lines and a high-level perspective of tectonic plate movement provides information about why earthquakes occur near major cities in the state, such as Los Angeles, which is set as the map's initial focal point.

To help the viewer interpret these data layers, a clean, white legend is positioned in the bottom-left corner. This legend clarifies the distinction between tectonic forces and the specific fault structures they create. Denoting the separation of off-shore and on-shore faults along with their movement can also help explain the severity of earthquakes and related disasters such as tsunamis and landslides. By combining these visual elements, the map transforms abstract geological data into a clear narrative about the constant motion and potential hazards inherent to the California coast.

Mapping California’s Recent Seismic Activity

To better understand earthquakes in California, it helps to examine where seismic activity has occurred over the past few decades. Mapping events from 2000 to 2025 reveals spatial patterns that are difficult to detect in raw datasets alone. Visualizing this information highlights how earthquake distribution relates to the state’s complex tectonic environment and interconnected fault systems, offering clearer insight into where and why earthquakes tend to occur.

The interactive map to the right illustrates these patterns by showing clusters of seismic activity across the state. Its legend categorizes earthquakes by magnitude, distinguishing frequent smaller events from rarer, more powerful ones. The results show that seismic activity is concentrated along major fault zones, including coastal and inland regions, demonstrating that earthquake risk in California is widespread rather than limited to a single fault.

Mapping Annualized Earthquake Loss Across California’s Counties

This interactive map focuses on visualizing Annualized Earthquake Loss (AEL) across the counties of California. Interactivity is a central feature of the map. When a user clicks on a county, a sidebar opens displaying detailed information, including the county name and its estimated annualized earthquake loss in U.S. dollars.

A horizontal bar indicator within the sidebar visually represents the relative magnitude of loss compared to a predefined maximum, helping users quickly interpret the severity of risk across different counties.

Altogether, the map effectively communicates how earthquake risk is distributed across California, emphasizing areas where potential economic impacts are greatest.

Conclusions and Strategic Findings

Spatial and Temporal Risk Concentration: Seismic risk in California is concentrated along major fault systems (San Andreas, Hayward, Walker Lane), with clustering occurring near high-density population centers.

Cyclical Tectonic Stress: Analysis of historical events reveals clear spatial patterns, showing earthquakes clustering near active faults and population centers, reinforcing the inevitability of future high-magnitude events despite periods of relative quiet.

Intersection of Vulnerability and Exposure: Connects historical disaster data to current infrastructure, demonstrating how past events have shaped modern regulatory frameworks and public awareness.

Call for Proactive Mitigation: Highlights that comprehensive mapping of the seismic landscape is essential for informing targeted policy interventions, building retrofits, and life-saving preparedness strategies.
